# How the Madina Mushaf divides juz, hizb and rubʿ: Ghayth al-Nafʿ

## The source

The Madina Mushaf (King Fahd Complex) states in its closing note that the starts of its 30
juz, 60 hizb and their quarters were taken from al-Ṣafāqusī's *Ghayth al-Nafʿ*:

> وأُخِذَ بيانُ أوائل أجزائه الثلاثين وأحزابه الستين وأرباعها من كتاب «غيث النفع» للعلامة السفاقُسيِّ …

The same note names further works for these divisions: *Nāẓimat al-Zahr* (al-Shāṭibī) with
its commentary, *Taḥqīq al-Bayān* (Muḥammad al-Mutawallī) and *Irshād al-Qurrāʾ wa-l-Kātibīn*
(Abū ʿĪd Riḍwān al-Mukhallalātī).

*Ghayth al-Nafʿ fī al-Qirāʾāt al-Sabʿ* is by ʿAlī al-Nūrī al-Ṣafāqusī of Sfax, Tunisia
(d. 1118 AH). It is a work on the seven readings, set out surah by surah. Along the way it marks where each
rubʿ, half-hizb and hizb ends.

## How the divisions came about

The divisions were set by scholars' own judgement (*ijtihādī*). They were not fixed by revelation
(*tawqīfī*), and that is why mushafs and regions differ on them.

1. **The Companions divided the Quran by surahs.** The seven *aḥzāb* (manāzil) were whole groups of surahs, for
   finishing the Quran in a week.
2. **Later division was by letter count.** From the time of al-Ḥajjāj (d. 95 AH), the letters were counted
   and the text was cut into halves, thirds, sevenths and so on, by letters. Works such as Ibn Abī Dāwūd's
   *al-Maṣāḥif* and al-Dānī's *al-Bayān* record these counts.
3. **The 30 juz, each of 2 hizb of 4 rubʿ, came out of the same counting.** That gives 30 juz, 60 hizb and 240 rubʿ.
   The count decides roughly where a division falls. The exact place is then chosen at a nearby aya end. As
   one survey puts it, the juz follow "the wording, not the meaning"
   (لوحظ … جانب اللفظ لا جانب المعنى), so a juz or rubʿ can open in the middle of a subject.
4. **The sources agree on the juz but differ on hizb and rubʿ.** Ghānim al-Ḥamad, *Taḥzīb al-Qurʾān fī
   al-Maṣādir wa-l-Maṣāḥif*, puts it this way: «مواضع الأحزاب وأرباعها كانت مجالا لتعدد الآراء». The
   Maghāriba (North Africa) and the Mashāriqa (the East) often place a rubʿ several ayat apart.
   Al-Ḥamad recommends Ghayth al-Nafʿ as the most careful basis.

## Al-Ṣafāqusī's method at a boundary

At each division end, Ghayth al-Nafʿ gives the same set of facts, in a fixed form:

| element | wording | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| the end word | {أليم}، {العالمين} | the last word of the portion |
| pause grade | تام | a complete stop: the next words do not depend on what came before |
| aya end | فاصلة بلا خلاف | an aya end in every counting school |
| the chosen end | ومنتهى الربع على ما اقتصر عليه في اللطائف / على المشهور | follows al-Qasṭallānī's *Laṭāʾif al-Ishārāt*, or the well-known view |
| the alternatives | … قبله لجميع المغاربة، … بعده لبعض المشارقة | where other traditions put the end |
| the preference | والأول أولى | his weighing of the views |

Two examples:

- **Al-Jāthiya.** He writes «وأليمٌ تام وفاصلة بلا خلاف ومنتهى الربع على ما اقتصر عليه في اللطائف».
  Here أليم is 45:11. The Maghāriba end before it, at «عظيم» (45:10). Some Mashāriqa end after it at
  «يتفكرون» (45:13), and most of them at «ترجعون» (45:15). He concludes «والأول أولى».
  **Madina Mushaf: the rubʿ starts at 45:12**, which is his preferred end.
- **At-Takwīr.** He writes «العالمين تام وفاصلة بلا خلاف ومنتهى نصف الحزب على المشهور، وقيل أحضرت قبله، وقيل آخر الانفطار».
  Here العالمين is 81:29. One alternative is «أحضرت» (81:14), and another is the end of Al-Infiṭār.
  **Madina Mushaf: the rubʿ starts at 82:1**, which is the well-known view.

So a traditional boundary satisfies four rules:

1. It is near the point a letter count gives.
2. It falls at an aya end that every counting school agrees on.
3. That aya end is a complete pause (*waqf tāmm*), or at least a sufficient one (*kāfī*).
4. Where traditions differ, it follows the well-known view, after the alternatives have been weighed.

Rule 1 is about size. Rules 2 to 4 are about meaning.

## What this means for Quran-Tajzee

| traditional rule | Quran-Tajzee |
|---|---|
| size by letter count | size by recitation (harakat with madd) and print width (DigitalKhatt), mixed 50/50 |
| an agreed aya end | every boundary is an aya end (Kufan count, Tanzil). Agreement across the counting schools is not yet checked. |
| a complete pause | a phrase start: a new subject (Task 1). A rubʿ that starts inside a phrase is at an aya end only, so it needs review. |
| the well-known view among alternatives | the traditional juz are kept as they are (Task 4). Inside them, the exact solver chooses. |

The juz are kept because their balance is already close to the limit that phrase granularity
allows (Task 3: ±6.2% traditional against ±5.0% optimised). The hizb and rubʿ are where the
traditional division is uneven: ±14% and ±35% of their share of the juz. See
[Task 4](../tasks/04-hizb-rub.md).

## Open points

- **A full table of Ghayth al-Nafʿ's 240 boundaries.** Online, only two of his passages could be read in full here.
  A table of every boundary would give the waqf grade at each traditional start. It would also give the
  Maghāriba and Mashāriqa alternatives, which are a set of boundaries that already have traditional
  standing. The solver could use them as preferred cut points.
- **Agreement across the counting schools (فاصلة بلا خلاف).** Each boundary could be checked against the counting schools'
  aya ends, so that no new boundary falls on an aya end that only the Kufan count has.
